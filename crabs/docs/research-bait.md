# How far bait pulls crab, and how fast they walk

Follow-up literature pass to answer two questions for the pot model: how far
away a crab notices a baited pot and comes to it, and how far crab move in a
day. Companion to [research.md](research.md). Tags: **strong** (several
studies agree), **moderate** (one good study or a sound inference),
**speculative** (a guess with no direct evidence).

## Headline

Nobody has measured the attraction radius of a baited pot for red king crab.
The best analog is a snow crab tag-and-recapture experiment (Brethes et al.
1985): crab notice a pot from roughly **100–140 m** and the area where they
are likely to be caught within a day reaches about **60 m**. Red king crab
studies cover how they approach (from downstream, locked onto the scent),
how often they get in (about 8% per approach), how catch grows with soak
time, and how fast they walk.

**Plume models.** Sainte-Marie & Hargrave 1987 (Mar. Biol. 94:431) is the
standard model: a Gaussian scent plume whose detectable length grows with
bait release rate and shrinks with current speed, scent threshold and
mixing. Faster current means a longer, narrower plume. Blue crab flume work
(Weissburg & Zimmer-Faust 1993, 1994) shows crab track scent reliably only
in smooth flow; turbulence makes paths indirect. No king-crab-specific plume
parameters exist.

Sex, season and temperature effects are only qualitative. Nobody has
measured walking speed as a function of hunger.

## C. How arrival depends on current and bait

- **Current points the plume.** Lab and in-situ video agree king crab come
  from downstream and search a narrow sector. A Gaussian plume stretches
  with current until turbulence breaks up the scent trail.
- **Bait.** Scent thresholds are very low (10⁻⁴–10⁻⁶ g/L). Soluble protein
  leaving the bait drops ~40% in the first hour. Nephrops studies show catch
  tracks *release rate* more than bait mass. No study relates bait mass to
  attraction radius for any crab.
- **Arrival curve.** In Bristol Bay, legal-male catch grew only 20% from 12
  to 24 h but doubled from 24 to 72 h, so pots were still filling at three
  days. Square pots in the Barents Sea levelled off at ~2 days. Even crab
  released 5 m from a pot were caught only 3–5% of the time in 1–2 days, so
  catch is limited by behaviour at the pot more than by plume reach.

## D. Proposed sim numbers

1. **Detection radius 120 m at 10 cm/s current**, growing roughly with
   current between 5 and 20 cm/s, capped at ~200 m, stretched downstream
   (a cone about ±67° around the downstream direction). *Moderate*.
2. **Effective catch radius 60 m per 24 h soak**, with catch probability
   falling from its maximum at the pot to zero at the detection radius.
   *Moderate*.
3. **Entry probability 0.08–0.10 per approach, 2–4 approaches per crab**, so
   a crab that arrives has a 20–30% chance of being caught. Calibrate so
   catch at 72 h ≈ 2× catch at 24 h ≈ 2.5× catch at 12 h. *Strong* for the
   soak ratios, *moderate* for entry probability.
4. **Walking speed toward bait 1–3 m/min**, bursts to 9 m/min. A crab 120 m
   downstream arrives in 1–2 hours; most arrivals from inside the radius
   happen in the first 6–12 hours, and later catch comes from crab wandering
   into the plume. *Moderate* for speeds, the arrival-time story is
   inference.
5. **Background movement 0.8 km/day net in spring** (drawn from 0.1–2.5),
   **0.2–0.5 km/day net in summer feeding mode** with ~1.5 km/day of path;
   slower in warm water. *Strong* for the spring number, *speculative* for
   the seasonal and temperature scaling.

## What this changes in the rules

- Rule 13 said catch "levels off by 48 h". Bristol Bay data say it is still
  doubling between 24 and 72 h. Revise to a slower curve.
- Rule 14's plume radius of "hundreds of metres" becomes 120–200 m.
- A fine patch only needs to extend a few hundred metres beyond the
  outermost pot in a string, not a whole 5 km cell.

## Gaps

- No bait-mass-to-radius relation for any crab.
