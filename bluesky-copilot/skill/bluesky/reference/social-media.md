# Reading social media well

Read this before explaining a post, a joke, a pile-on or a community dispute, and before answering "is this real?". It covers how people use Bluesky, which the API cannot tell you.

## How posts relate to each other

- **Reply**: a response attached under a post. Replies under a big post are mostly from strangers and skew toward whoever is annoyed or excited enough to write.
- **Repost**: sharing a post unchanged. Usually approval or "look at this", but it is not a statement in the reposter's words.
- **Quote post**: sharing a post with a comment on top. The comment is the point. People quote to agree and add, to joke, to disagree, or to hold a post up for ridicule (a "dunk"). The quoted author's view and the quoter's view are different things, and are often opposed.
- **Quote chain**: a quote of a quote of a quote. The top post may be several steps removed from what started it. See the quote-chain recipe in `SKILL.md`.
- **Chain game**: a quote chain where everyone repeats a prompt ("quote this with your…") and adds an answer. It is play, not argument.
- **Thread**: one author replying to themselves to say more than fits in a post. Read the whole thread before characterizing the first post.
- **Ratio**: a post with many more replies or quotes than likes. People are mostly responding to object.
- **Pile-on**: many accounts quoting or replying to one person at once. The target may be a public figure or a stranger who went viral by accident. Describe what happened; do not add to it, and do not dig into a private person's history.
- **Subtweet** (the word carried over from Twitter): a post about someone or something without naming it ("some of you need to hear this"). You often cannot know the target. Say so; offer the likely context only when other posts from the same hour make it plain, and say it is an inference.
- **Vague posting**: an emotional post with no stated cause ("I am so sick of this"). Check the author's nearby posts and replies for the cause. If none appears, say it is not stated.
- **Screenshot**: a picture of a post from another site, or of a deleted or blocked post. A screenshot proves nothing by itself: it can be edited, cropped or out of date. Look for the original.
- **Detached or deleted quote**: the quoted author removed their post from someone's quote, or deleted it. People do this to stop a pile-on.
- **Alt text**: the author's description of an image. On Bluesky people also use it for the punchline or extra commentary, so read it.

## Registers that are easy to misread

- **Irony and bits.** Much of Bluesky is deadpan. A post that states something absurd in a flat voice is usually a joke. Running jokes ("bits") only make sense with the earlier posts; check the author's recent posts and the quoted post.
- **Satire outlets and parody accounts.** They write invented news in a straight voice. Check the account's bio and display name for "parody", "satire", "fan" or "not affiliated", and compare with its other posts. A real event and a satire of it often circulate on the same day.
- **Memes and formats.** A recurring image or phrasing with swapped-in text. The meaning comes from the format. If you recognize the format, name it; if not, describe what you see and search for the phrase (see `resources.md`).
- **Engagement bait.** "Name a movie that…", outrage with no source, "like if you agree". Low information; say so if asked what it is.
- **Reply guys, sealioning, concern trolling.** Terms people use for unwelcome repliers. Report that the term was used; do not apply it yourself.
- **In-group shorthand.** Communities have their own words. Explain a term only if you can find it used consistently, or a resource confirms it. Otherwise say you are not sure.
- **Bluesky's own vocabulary.** "Skeet" (a post, jokingly), "the atmosphere" (apps built on the same protocol), "labeler", "starter pack", "Discover", "moots" or "mutuals" (people who follow each other).

## Describing a dispute fairly

- Give the sequence: who posted what first, who responded, with links. Use each side's own words.
- Size it honestly. Three accounts with a few likes each are not "the community". Say how many posts you read and how much engagement they had.
- Separate the argument from the people. Describe positions, not character. Do not rank who "won".
- Do not repeat accusations about a named person as fact. Attribute them ("X says that Y did…") and say whether you found any support.
- Leave private individuals who are being piled on out of it as far as you can: describe the post, not the person.

## Checking whether something is real

A short routine, in this order. Stop as soon as you have a solid answer.

1. **Who is saying it?** Check the account: bio, handle domain, verification, follower count, age, what else it posts. A newsroom's handle is usually its own website's domain.
2. **Is it meant seriously?** Rule out satire, parody and jokes.
3. **Where does the claim come from?** Follow the link and read it. See whether the post's claim matches what the page says; headlines and posts often overstate. No source at all is itself worth reporting.
4. **Does anyone independent confirm it?** Look for at least one established news organization reporting the same thing, on Bluesky or by web search. Many accounts repeating one original post is one source, not many.
5. **For images and video**: look at it. Check whether the caption matches what is visible, and whether signs, weather, dates or text in the image fit the claim. Old pictures are often reposted as new, and generated or edited images circulate too. You cannot certify a picture as authentic by eye; lean on steps 3 and 4.
6. **Is it current?** Check the dates of the post, the article and the event. A true story from years ago shared as news is misleading.

Report the result at its real strength:

- **Confirmed**: an identified, credible source reports it, and you read that source.
- **Satire or a joke**: say what gives it away.
- **Unverified**: you found the claim but no independent support. Say where you looked.
- **Misleading**: the source exists but says something different, or the item is old or out of context. Say what the source really says.
- **False**: a credible source directly contradicts it. Name the source.

Do not turn "I found nothing" into "it's fake", or a confident tone into "it's true".
