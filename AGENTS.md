# Writing guidance

Apply this guidance to responses, explanations, reports, plans, and documentation. Help the reader understand the point on the first reading. Spend words on context and connections; remove words that do neither.

1. **Supply the context the reader needs.** Include enough background to understand the current point without reconstructing an earlier conversation. Avoid retelling the whole history.

2. **Ground unfamiliar ideas in specifics.** Explain what happens, how parts relate, or why a choice fits its goals and constraints. Use an example, mechanism, or tradeoff according to the reader's question. Connect those specifics to the broader idea.

3. **Name who or what acts and what changes.** Identify the person, component, or process performing an action and what it affects. Prefer "The sensor checks whether the tank is full" to "Capacity verification occurs." Show when responsibility passes between actors.

4. **Explain how ideas connect.** Say what caused a result, what an example demonstrates, or why a distinction matters. Headings and adjacent sentences cannot replace missing reasoning.

5. **Use ordinary words where they preserve meaning.** Prefer "use" to "utilize." Keep technical terms when they add precision, and explain unfamiliar ones when introducing them. A theory's name or a citation supports an explanation; it does not replace one.

6. **Make references unambiguous.** Repeat the relevant object, action, or option when "this" or "the former" could mean several things. A little repetition can prevent the reader from having to look backward.

7. **Use structure to reveal meaning.** Distinguish decisions, proposals, examples, and unfinished work. Write descriptive headings. Use lists for items the reader needs to inspect separately and prose for reasoning that develops across sentences.

8. **Place qualifications where they matter.** State assumptions before relying on them. Label hypothetical examples. Distinguish evidence from interpretation and identify whose judgment you report. Explain a qualification once where it applies.

9. **Let length serve understanding.** Expand compressed sentences when they hide actors or connections. Remove repetition that adds no meaning. Avoid forcing every sentence to be short or minimizing word count at the expense of clarity.

10. **Use a natural speaking voice.** Choose familiar verbs and contractions that fit the audience. Let sentences carry the explanation. Reserve emphasis for information the reader needs to find quickly.

11. **Preserve meaning while simplifying.** Keep conditions, uncertainty, optionality, and attribution intact. Do not turn "can" into "will," a proposal into a decision, or an objective into a mandatory stopping point.

## Review before delivering

Read as someone who understands the audience's vocabulary but has not followed the work. Check:

- Is the main point clear, including why it matters?
- Is it clear who or what acts, what changes, and how the steps connect?
- Are unfamiliar terms explained and references unambiguous?
- Can the reader distinguish facts, assumptions, proposals, decisions, and open questions?
- Does each example help explain the idea?
- Has simpler wording preserved the claim?

Revise phrases the reader must mentally translate. Add missing connections and remove explanations that repeat work already done.
